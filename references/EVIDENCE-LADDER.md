# The Evidence Ladder (how strong is the proof, really?)

Discovery in SKILL.md tells you whether a pain is real and badly served. It reads what people **say**. This file is about the stronger kind of proof: what people **do**. Pull it in at the end of Phase 0 (to name the rung), in Phase 6 (to list the assumptions and pick the test), and whenever someone asks "is this validated yet?"

Three tools live here: the ladder, the assumption list, and the size-and-price check.

## The ladder: five rungs, weakest to strongest

Saying is cheap. Doing costs something. Each rung up costs the other person more, which is exactly why it proves more.

| Rung | What you have | What it proves | What it does not prove |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. **People complain** | Verified quotes from Reddit and reviews (Phase 0) | The pain exists and people feel it | That they'd do anything about it |
| 2. **People work around it** | They already spend time or money on a clumsy fix: a spreadsheet, a freelancer, a paid tool they grumble about | The pain is bad enough to act on | That they'd pick *your* fix |
| 3. **People sign up** | Strangers gave an email, replied to a DM, booked a call | Your framing of the answer pulls people in | That they'd pay |
| 4. **People commit** | A pre-order, a deposit, a signed letter, a calendar slot they kept | They'll put something real on the line | That it works at scale |
| 5. **People pay** | Money changed hands for the thing | It's a business, at least a small one | That it grows |

**Rules for reading it:**

- **A rung only counts with evidence you can point at.** A link, a screenshot, a number. "My friends said they'd pay" is not rung 4, it's not on the ladder at all (see the friends-and-family catch in SKILL.md).
- **Desk research tops out at rung 2.** Phase 0 can earn rung 1 (verified complaints) and rung 2 (visible workarounds and money already moving). Rungs 3 to 5 need a real-world test. That's what [TEST-KIT.md](TEST-KIT.md) is for.
- **Don't skip rungs in your claims, do skip them in your tests.** If the fastest honest test is a pre-order page, run it. You don't have to collect emails first.
- **Strangers count, friends don't.** People who love you will lie to be kind. A rung is climbed by people with no reason to be nice.

**Say it to the user in plain words.** "Right now you're on rung 2: people complain about this, and they're already paying for clumsy fixes. That's a real start, and it's further than most ideas get. Rung 3 is the next one: get ten strangers to raise a hand. Here's the cheapest way to do it."

Name the rung in three places: the findings summary, the plan's Riskiest Assumption section, and the Verdict Card (see [DIAGRAM-SYSTEM.md](DIAGRAM-SYSTEM.md)).

## The assumption list (every belief, not just one)

An idea is a stack of beliefs. Most of them are untested. The riskiest-assumption test in Phase 6 picks one, but you can only pick the right one if you've seen the whole pile.

List them in four plain groups:

- **Want it.** These people have this problem, and badly enough to change what they do.
- **Find it.** You can reach them, in a place you can name, at a cost you can afford.
- **Pay for it.** Somebody pays, enough, often enough.
- **Build it.** Your AI tool can actually make it work, and it keeps working.

Then give each one two quick ratings:

- **How bad if it's wrong?** Fatal, painful, or annoying.
- **How much proof do we have?** Use the same tags as discovery: seen it, hunch, guess.

| Assumption | Group | If wrong | Proof |
|---|---|---|---|
| Freelancers hate writing the chase email | Want it | Fatal | seen it |
| They'll forward invoices to a new tool | Want it | Fatal | guess |
| They'll pay $9 a month | Pay for it | Painful | hunch |
| An AI draft sounds like them | Build it | Painful | guess |

**The riskiest assumption is the one that's fatal and still a guess.** That's the row the cheap test goes after. If two rows tie, pick the one that's cheaper to test.

Keep the list short for a beginner: six to eight rows, the ones that matter. For a confident user, go to twelve. The list goes into the plan's Riskiest Assumption section and gets a fresh look every time a test comes back.

## The size-and-price check (is it big enough to bother?)

Let the confidence dial decide whether this runs. A first-timer building a learning project can skip it. Anyone who wants real users, real money, or an investor's attention gets it, because it's the first thing they'll be asked.

No market reports. Three honest numbers, from the bottom up:

1. **How many of these people can you actually count?** Not "the freelance economy." The members of the three subreddits you mined, the reviewers on the tools you read, the listings in the directory you found. Write the count and where it came from.
2. **What does the pain cost them today?** The price of the clumsy fix (the tool, the freelancer, the hours). This is the ceiling on what you can charge. Nobody pays more to fix a problem than the problem costs.
3. **What do the closest tools charge?** Real prices from real pricing pages. This is the anchor people will judge you against.

Then one line of arithmetic, said out loud and held loosely: "If one in a hundred of those people paid about what the workaround costs, that's roughly ___ a month." The number isn't a forecast. It's a smell test with three outcomes:

- **Clearly enough** for what this person wants (a side income, a small business, a venture bet). Carry on.
- **Clearly too small.** Say so kindly, and offer the same three moves as a no-go: widen the group, raise the price by solving a costlier pain, or stop.
- **Can't tell.** Write it down as an open question. That's an honest answer too.

Every number carries its source, the same as a quote does. A count you can't point at is a guess, so tag it as one.
